Who I am

About me

I'm a financial engineer in the making, currently completing my MSc in Financial Engineering at WorldQuant University, with an MBA in Finance from the African School of Economics. My work sits at the intersection of markets and code — I like turning a hunch about how prices behave into something testable, then finding out whether it actually holds up.

That instinct is what led to my thesis: a systematic, market-neutral equity pairs trading strategy that uses machine learning for signal generation and risk management. It's the kind of project where the fun part isn't the idea — it's the part where you stress-test it and it either survives or it doesn't.

I work primarily in Python and R, with a solid grounding in financial econometrics, stochastic modelling, and risk management. Outside of models and backtests, I care about markets in the way some people care about puzzles — I want to know why something moved, not just that it did.

I'm currently looking for opportunities in Sales & Trading, with a particular interest in derivatives and systematic strategies. This portfolio is where I share the projects that got me here.

Yield Curve & Market Risk Analysis

Modeling the U.S. Treasury yield curve and decomposing market risk through correlation analysis — from raw FRED data to a fitted Nelson-Siegel curve and PCA-driven factor decomposition.

Python Nelson-Siegel Model PCA/SVD
  • Yield curve modeling — pulled historical U.S. Treasury yield data via the FRED API and fitted a Nelson-Siegel curve to model the term structure at different points in time, comparing fitted curves against observed yields.
  • Correlation & dimensionality reduction — used PCA and SVD to decompose the covariance structure of Treasury-linked ETF returns, isolating the dominant factors driving yield movements and demonstrating how a small number of components explain most of the variance in rate changes.
  • Empirical ETF analysis — applied the same PCA/SVD framework to a real estate sector ETF (XLRE) and its top 30 holdings, extracting principal components from daily return covariance to understand the sector's underlying risk factors.

Financial Econometrics: Diagnosing Data Pathologies

A practical framework for spotting and fixing the four statistical issues that quietly break financial models — skewness, fat tails, outlier sensitivity, and non-stationarity — with diagnosis and remedies for each.

Python Statsmodels Time Series
  • Skewness — simulated and measured distributional asymmetry, and discussed its impact on risk models that assume normality.
  • Kurtosis & heteroscedasticity — examined fat-tailed distributions and time-varying volatility, both common in financial returns and both dangerous when ignored in options pricing and risk management.
  • Outlier sensitivity — used the modified Z-score (median absolute deviation) to detect and quantify the distortion outliers introduce into means, variances, and regression coefficients.
  • Non-stationarity & unit root testing — applied the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test to detect random-walk behavior in time series, and discussed why modeling non-stationary data without differencing produces spurious, unreliable results.

Group project completed as part of the WorldQuant University MScFE program (Financial Econometrics module) — equal contribution across all components.

Cointegration, Non-Stationarity & Multicollinearity in Financial Time Series

From feature engineering on time series to a full Johansen cointegration study of gold, silver, and platinum — building a VECM model and diagnosing multicollinearity across major tech stocks along the way.

Python VECM Cointegration Time Series
  • Feature extraction — engineered lag features, rolling statistics, and differencing on time series data to prepare it for modeling, and assessed which transformations actually preserve useful signal.
  • Non-stationarity & long-run equilibrium — tested gold, silver, and platinum prices (1985–2024) for unit roots using ADF and KPSS tests, confirmed a cointegrating relationship via the Johansen test, and built a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM) capturing the long-run equilibrium between the three metals — the same statistical foundation used in pairs trading, where cointegrated assets are traded against each other on the assumption their prices revert to equilibrium.
  • Multicollinearity — analyzed daily returns of AAPL, MSFT, GOOGL, and AMZN, using correlation matrices and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) to detect and quantify redundancy between highly correlated tech stocks, identifying which pairs would destabilize a regression model.

Group project completed as part of the WorldQuant University MScFE program (Financial Econometrics module) — equal contribution across all components.

Option Pricing: Binomial Trees & Put-Call Parity

Pricing European and American options with the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein binomial model, then stress-testing put-call parity across moneyness levels to see where the theory breaks down for early-exercise options.

Python Binomial Trees Options Pricing
  • Binomial tree pricing — implemented the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (CRR) model with 100 time steps to price at-the-money European calls and puts, computing up/down factors and risk-neutral probabilities from volatility and the risk-free rate.
  • Moneyness sensitivity — priced calls and puts across a range of strikes (deep ITM to deep OTM) to confirm option values move as theory predicts: call prices fall as strikes rise, put prices rise as strikes rise.
  • Put-call parity testing — checked the parity relationship (C − P = S − PV(K)) across strikes for both European and American options, finding it held only at-the-money for European options and broke down elsewhere — and testing why American options systematically violate it due to early-exercise rights.
  • Multi-step binomial trees — extended the model to a 3-step tree pricing a put option, tracing asset price evolution node by node and discounting expected payoffs back to present value.

Group project completed as part of the WorldQuant University MScFE Derivative Pricing module — equal contribution across all components.

Exotic Option Pricing: Stochastic Volatility & Jump Diffusion

Pricing options under the Heston and Merton models via Monte Carlo simulation, extending to American early-exercise and barrier options — where theory meets the messiness of real option pricing.

Python Monte Carlo Heston Model Options Pricing
  • Heston model (stochastic volatility) — simulated correlated stock price and variance processes via Monte Carlo (10,000 paths) to price at-the-money European calls and puts, testing sensitivity to the correlation parameter ρ between asset returns and volatility.
  • Merton jump-diffusion model — modeled sudden price jumps (Poisson-distributed) layered on top of standard diffusion, pricing options under varying jump intensities and comparing the resulting price curves against the Heston model's smoother, volatility-driven behavior.
  • Greeks estimation — computed Delta and Gamma numerically under both models to quantify option price sensitivity to the underlying asset.
  • American options & early exercise — priced an American call under the Heston model using Monte Carlo with Least Squares Regression (the Longstaff-Schwartz approach), confirming the early-exercise premium against the European price.
  • Barrier options — priced Up-and-In Call and Down-and-In Put exotic options under jump-diffusion, showing how barrier conditions can significantly discount an option relative to its vanilla European equivalent.

Group project completed as part of the WorldQuant University MScFE Derivative Pricing module — equal contribution across all components.

Calibrating Stochastic Models to Real Market Data

Fitting the Heston, Bates, and CIR models to real option and interest-rate data — from stochastic volatility calibration via Fourier pricing to simulating a full year of interest-rate paths.

Python Heston Model Monte Carlo Calibration
  • Heston model calibration — calibrated the Heston stochastic volatility model to real 15-day equity option prices using Fourier-based pricing (the Lewis/Carr-Madan approach) and global optimization (differential evolution), converting puts to synthetic calls via put-call parity and verifying the Feller condition on the fitted parameters.
  • Bates model (Heston + jumps) — extended the calibration to 60-day options using the Bates model, which layers a jump-diffusion component on top of stochastic volatility, to test whether adding jumps improves the fit to market prices.
  • CIR interest rate model — calibrated the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model to the Euribor term structure (7 to 360 days), then simulated 100,000 one-year interest rate paths to estimate the expected 12-month Euribor rate and its 5th-95th percentile confidence range.

Group project completed as part of the WorldQuant University MScFE Stochastic Modeling module — equal contribution across all components.
